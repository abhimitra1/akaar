PATHS: A Physical–Digital–Physical Framework for Industry 5.0 Augmentation of Traditional Pottery Craft

Convergence of generative AI, digital twins and hybrid reality in service of the artisan's hand — architecture, pedagogy, and a pre-registered pilot protocol

Prof. Mukti Kanta Mishra1  ·  Abhi Mitra2
1 Centurion University of Technology and Management, Odisha, India   —   Corresponding author
2 Independent Researcher — System Architecture, Workflow Design and Implementation
Technical Support: Sunny Singh

Abstract

Traditional pottery and terracotta clusters in India sustain an estimated 6.47 million handloom and handicraft artisans [27], yet the makers capture a minority of final value because trader intermediaries function as the sole market interface [29]. Prevailing digital interventions either bypass the artisan entirely — substituting machine production for the hand — or stop at e-commerce listing, which changes the shelf but not the structure. This paper introduces PATHS (Pottery · Art · Technology · Heritage & Sustainability), a reference framework in which computational capability is deployed strictly upstream and downstream of the making act, never inside it. PATHS is organised as a Physical–Digital–Physical (PDP) cycle instantiated across six operational stages and twelve pedagogical stages, supported by four technology layers: multimodal and generative vision AI, a digital-twin and digital-thread substrate, augmented and hybrid reality, and computer-vision verification. We formalise the framework's central economic mechanism — the digital multiplier, a constrained combinatorial expansion of one artisan-authored master model along six orthogonal variation axes under invariant-preservation constraints — and show that it yields catalogue breadth without craft dilution. We map the framework against the European Commission's three Industry 5.0 pillars [1] and six Sustainable Development Goals, and derive its pedagogical instantiation as a multi-track university skill course. Because PATHS is pre-deployment, we make no empirical claims of our own; instead we assemble the effect sizes reported in adjacent literature — 82% assembly-error reduction under spatially registered AR [6], 94.9–98.0% accuracy in deep-learning ceramic defect inspection [9][10] — and specify a falsifiable twelve-month cluster pilot with six pre-registered hypotheses, defined metrics, control conditions, and stated failure criteria. The contribution is threefold: an architecture that is human-centric by construction rather than by assertion; a formal treatment of variation-under-constraint as a heritage-preserving mechanism; and an evaluation protocol that makes the framework refutable.

1 Introduction

The question that organises this paper is narrow and awkward: what can a computer legitimately do for a potter?

The answer that Industry 4.0 supplies — automate the throwing, standardise the form, scale the output — destroys the thing being sold. A hand-thrown pot commands its price precisely because of the irregularities that automation exists to eliminate — the craftsman's knowledge is embodied in the hand and is not separable from it [35]. Any intervention that industrialises the making act converts a craft good into a commodity good and competes it against injection-moulded ceramics at a price the artisan cannot survive.

The answer that digital commerce supplies is thinner but safer: photograph the pot, list it, ship it. This is not wrong, but it leaves the structural problem untouched. The artisan still makes speculatively against guessed demand, still finances unsold inventory, still receives a brief that is verbal and vague, still remains anonymous behind the platform's brand. The intermediary has been replaced, not removed.

PATHS proceeds from a third position. The making act is ring-fenced — throwing, glazing, firing and judgement remain wholly human and are treated as an inviolable boundary of the system. Computation is admitted only in the two places where the artisan is currently disadvantaged: specification (converting a distant customer's vague intent into an unambiguous, dimensioned, pre-approved target) and verification (proving the fired piece matches that target, so the maker is paid without dispute). Everything between those two moments belongs to the hand.

This is not a rhetorical constraint but an architectural one, and it has consequences throughout the design. It determines that AI generates designs, not objects. It determines that AR is a reference overlay, not a robotic guide. It determines that computer vision measures deviation for settlement, not for rejection-by-tolerance. Section 3 makes the constraint explicit; Sections 4–6 show what follows from it.

1.1 Contributions

  1. A boundary-first architecture. A four-layer reference architecture (§4) in which the human-inviolable zone is a first-class design object, not an ethical footnote — technology capabilities are admitted or excluded by their relationship to that boundary.
  2. Formalisation of the digital multiplier. A constrained combinatorial model (§5) of how one artisan-authored master expands into a large variant family while preserving a defined invariant set. We give the cardinality expression, the constraint semantics, and the mechanism by which authorship survives multiplication.
  3. A structural value model. An analysis (§6) of which links in the craft value chain PATHS actually alters — order arrival, payment timing, inventory risk, rework, buyer identity, price setting — separated from claims about magnitude, which we do not make.
  4. A refutable pilot protocol. Six pre-registered hypotheses with operational metrics, control conditions, sample frame and explicit failure criteria (§9), so that the framework can be shown to be wrong.
  5. A pedagogical instantiation. A four-track university skill course (§8) that treats the cluster as the laboratory and produces a verified artefact as its assessment object.

2 Background and Related Work

2.1 Industry 5.0 and the human-centric turn

The European Commission's 2021 policy brief defines Industry 5.0 through three pillars — human-centricity, sustainability and resilience — explicitly repositioning the worker from a cost to be minimised to a stakeholder whose wellbeing is an objective of the production system [1]. Romero and Stahre extend this to the operational layer with the Resilient Operator 5.0, arguing that system resilience is downstream of operator resilience and that computing environments should therefore be symbiotic and cognitive rather than merely instructive [2]. The distinction that matters for craft is the one Industry 5.0 draws by inversion: Industry 4.0 asks what a machine can do without a person; Industry 5.0 asks what a person can do with a machine. PATHS is constructed entirely on the second question, and §7 tests the framework against each pillar in turn.

2.2 Digital twins and the digital thread

Grieves introduced the digital twin as a triad of physical product, virtual product, and the bidirectional connection between them [3]; Tao and colleagues extended this to a five-dimensional formulation adding twin data and services [4], and subsequent reviews have consolidated the concept's enabling technologies and industrial applications [5]. Almost all of this literature assumes a machine-executed physical process, where the twin's purpose is predictive control or simulation-driven optimisation. Craft breaks that assumption: the physical process is a human one, is not deterministic, and is not meant to be. PATHS therefore uses the twin in a narrower and less usual role — as a contractual artefact. The twin is the shared, unambiguous statement of what was agreed, against which the fired piece is later measured. Its function is agreement and settlement rather than control.

2.3 Augmented reality as work guidance

The empirical basis for AR-as-guidance is unusually well established for a topic in this space. Tang et al.'s CHI 2003 between-subjects study found that spatially registered AR instructions reduced assembly error rates by 82% relative to printed manuals, with the largest effect on cumulative errors propagating from earlier mistakes [6]. More recent work in Industry 4.0 operator settings reports significant reductions in both completion time and error count [7], though evidence is mixed for head-mounted displays as task complexity rises and workload measures often remain comparable to paper baselines [8]. PATHS reads this literature carefully rather than optimistically: the well-supported claim is that spatial registration of a 3D target onto the workpiece reduces divergence from that target, and that is exactly and only what stage 4 of our framework asks of AR.

2.4 Generative image-to-3D reconstruction

Single-image 3D reconstruction has moved from research curiosity to deployable component within roughly two years. Feed-forward transformer reconstructors such as TripoSR [20] and sparse-view mesh generators such as InstantMesh [21] produce usable meshes in seconds; generation–reconstruction cycle methods improve multi-view consistency [23]; and high-fidelity asset pipelines now target production-grade detail [22]. For PATHS this capability is load-bearing but not sufficient — a generated mesh is a proposal, not a design. It becomes a design only after the artisan has constrained it to what clay, wheel and kiln permit, which is why stage 2 places a craft-feasibility filter between generation and twinning.

2.5 Computer-vision verification

Automated ceramic inspection is mature enough to serve as a settlement instrument. A real-time CNN-based defect detection system for ceramic manufacturing reported 98.00% accuracy and an F1 score of 97.29% [9]; reviews of deep learning for ceramic surface defects document robust performance across glaze shrinkage, spotting, bubbling, cracking, pinholes and scratches under varied illumination [10], and decorative-ceramic inspection work reports 94.90% test accuracy at 25 fps [10]. PATHS inverts the conventional use of this capability. In industry, CV inspection exists to reject. In PATHS it exists to certify: the mesh-deviation report is evidence that the maker delivered what was commissioned, and the residual deviation is the piece's handmade signature rather than a defect.

2.6 Co-creation, mass customisation, and craft heritage

Prahalad and Ramaswamy located value creation in the interaction between firm and customer rather than in the firm alone [15]; Vargo and Lusch's service-dominant logic generalised this, reducing the producer's role to that of value-proposition maker with the customer as co-creator [16]. PATHS adds a third party that both literatures under-theorise: the artisan is neither firm nor customer, and holds authorship rights that neither framework assigns. Mass customisation supplies the operational counterpart — Pine's original formulation [17] and its recent AI-era revisions [18] establish that pull-based make-to-order production suppresses forecast error and finished-goods inventory, which in a craft cluster is the difference between the potter financing unsold stock and not. On the heritage side, systematic reviews of 3D technologies for intangible cultural heritage [11][14] and formal methods for digitising craft processes as distinct from craft objects [12] establish both the feasibility and the standing critique: digitisation can preserve the artefact while stranding the practice — a concern central to the UNESCO safeguarding framework, which locates heritage in living transmission rather than in the object [34]. Recent work on digital creation models for intangible-heritage crafts argues that the digital layer must generate new demand rather than merely new records [13]. PATHS responds by routing demand back to living clusters, so the archive is a by-product of continued making rather than a substitute for it.

2.7 Research gap

Gap statement

Each capability above is individually mature. What is absent is an integrated framework that (a) composes them into a closed physical–digital–physical loop, (b) treats the human making act as an architectural invariant rather than an optimisation target, (c) assigns authorship and margin to the artisan by construction, and (d) states in advance what evidence would refute it. PATHS is proposed to fill that gap.

3 The PATHS Framework

3.1 The Physical–Digital–Physical cycle

The framework's organising primitive is a cycle that begins and ends in physical reality, passing through a digital phase whose only justification is what it returns to the physical. This ordering is deliberate and distinguishes PATHS from digital-first craft platforms, where the physical object is an output of a digital catalogue. Here the digital phase is an instrument of the physical one.

3.2 The six-stage operational journey

Operationally the cycle resolves into six stages. Stages 1–3 are digital and customer-facing; stages 4–6 are physical and artisan-facing. The boundary between stage 3 and stage 4 is the framework's most important seam: it is the point at which a negotiable proposal becomes a committed, paid-for target, and it is also the point at which the customer's authority ends and the artisan's begins.

3.3 The twelve-stage Learning Loop

The six operational stages are a delivery description. For teaching and for research they are too coarse: several stages compress two distinct cognitive acts into one label. The Learning Loop unfolds them into twelve stages arranged in three phases, and reframes the cycle as a design-methodological one in the sense of design thinking as an iterative, human-centred discipline [19] — Design Thinking — Thinking Design. The first direction moves from observed need to designed artefact; the second moves from the made artefact back to a revised understanding of what should have been designed. Every turn of the loop terminates where it began, at observing.

The inviolable zone

Stages 10–11 (Re-casting and Crafting) constitute the framework's human-only zone. No PATHS component may automate, actuate, or mechanically assist within it. The constraint is economic as much as ethical: the handmade character is the product, so automating it destroys the good being sold. This is why we describe PATHS as human-centric by construction — the boundary is enforced by what the architecture omits, not by a policy layered on top of it.

4 Reference Architecture

PATHS composes four capability layers. Layers are ordered by their distance from the physical: the intelligence layer never touches an object, the verification layer touches nothing else. Geometry moves between layers through open interchange formats — glTF for web delivery, USDZ for mobile AR [24] — so that no cluster is locked to a single vendor's pipeline. Each layer is admitted to the architecture only where it can be shown to sit outside the inviolable zone of §3.3.

5 The Digital Multiplier

The economic weakness of one-of-one commissioned craft is catalogue thinness. A cluster that can show a buyer forty forms will lose to a factory that can show four thousand. The conventional resolution — hire designers to produce variants — severs the link between the maker and the design, and is the mechanism by which craft becomes craft-styled manufacture.

PATHS resolves this differently. A single artisan-authored master model is expanded computationally along orthogonal variation axes, subject to a constraint set that preserves what makes the piece the artisan's. We call the resulting operator the digital multiplier.

5.1 Formalisation

Let M be the master model — a 3D geometry authored or ratified by the artisan, carrying an invariant set I(M) consisting of characteristic proportion ratios, the regional motif grammar, and the maker's signature features. Let A = {a1 … a6} be the six variation axes, each ai admitting ki discrete options. The raw variant space is the Cartesian product:

V(M) = a1 × a2 × … × a6,   |V| = ∑i=1..6 ki (1)

This product is not the deliverable. Two filters are applied. First, a craft-feasibility predicate φ(v) rejects variants that clay, wheel or kiln cannot realise — wall-thickness minima, overhang limits, shrinkage tolerance, kiln-envelope dimensions. Second, an invariance predicate ι(v) rejects variants in which the master's characteristic ratios, motif grammar or signature features have been lost. The admissible family is:

V*(M) = { v ∈ V(M)  :  φ(v) ∧ ι(v) },   |V*| = ρ·|V|,  ρ ∈ (0,1] (2)

where ρ is the joint retention ratio. The design claim of the framework is that ρ should be learned from the cluster, not assumed: every completed commission contributes a measured deviation record (§3.1) that tightens φ, and every artisan rejection in stage 3 supplies a labelled negative example that tightens ι. The multiplier therefore becomes more faithful to a given cluster's practice the longer that cluster uses it.

Why multiplication does not dilute

The distinction between the digital multiplier and generic generative design is the predicate ι. A generative system unconstrained by ι converges on the training distribution's mean — it produces plausible pottery, which is to say pottery belonging to no one. Constrained by ι, every member of V* inherits the master's proportions, motif grammar and signature, and every member is attributable to the artisan who authored M. Breadth is generated in the axes; identity is held constant in the invariants. This is the mechanism by which a cluster can present thousands of options without any of them ceasing to be its own.

6 Artisan Value Model

India's handicraft sector recorded exports of ₹33,122.79 crore in 2024–25 and supports an estimated 64.66 lakh (6.47 million) handloom and handicraft artisans, with women constituting roughly 64% of the total [27][28]. Yet cluster-level studies consistently report that in rural craft clusters market channels remain underdeveloped and traders operate as the sole "market makers," extracting substantial margin while paying artisans low piece rates [29]; ethnographic work on terracotta clusters in West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh documents the resulting living conditions in detail [30][31].

We are careful here about what kind of claim we are making. PATHS does not assert a specific margin uplift — no such number can be honestly produced before deployment. What can be stated in advance is structural: which links in the chain the framework alters, and by what mechanism. Table 3 sets this out; §9 specifies how the magnitudes would be measured.

6.1 Evidence available from adjacent domains

On the customer side, AR product display has been shown to reduce product uncertainty and improve product attitudes [25], and reviews of augmented shopping behaviour report consistent gains in evaluation clarity [26] — relevant here because a commissioned one-of-one is precisely the case where a buyer cannot inspect before purchase. Because PATHS is pre-deployment, the honest evidentiary position is to state what the component technologies have been shown to do elsewhere, and to treat those as upper-bound plausibility arguments rather than predictions. 6.2 Design commitments

Value routed to the artisan is a design decision, not an emergent property. The following commitments are treated as architectural requirements and are auditable:

  1. Named authorship. Every piece and every derived variant carries the maker's name in the provenance record. Anonymous stock is not representable in the data model.
  2. No skill extraction. Captured process data may be used to tighten feasibility constraints (φ) but may not be used to train generative systems that reproduce an artisan's hand without them.
  3. Paid before making. Funds are released at stage-3 ratification, not on delivery or on sale.
  4. Shared capital. One tablet and one scanning rig per cluster; no artisan is required to purchase hardware to participate.
  5. Refusal rights. An artisan may decline any commission at stage 3 without penalty or ranking effect.
  6. Transparent price split. The maker's share, platform fee and logistics cost are disclosed to both artisan and buyer on every order.

The 4.0 — 5.0 inversion

Industry 4.0 asks what a machine can do without a person. Industry 5.0 asks what a person can do with a machine. Applied to craft the difference is not philosophical but commercial: the first question produces a cheaper pot that competes with injection moulding and loses; the second produces a commissioned pot that competes with nothing.

8 Pedagogical Instantiation

A framework of this kind is only testable if someone builds it, and the most durable way to have it built is to make it a curriculum. PATHS is therefore instantiated as a multi-track skill course — Industry 5.0 in Traditional Art — delivered with a partner potter cluster. The sequencing is deliberate and unusual: students learn the craft before they are permitted to build anything for it.

The learning outcomes are stated in the same register as the framework's ethics: engineers who can apply AI, 3D and AR to a living craft rather than to a benchmark; managers who can construct phygital customer experience around a one-of-one object; graduates who leave with a verified artefact and documented cluster fieldwork. The twelve-stage Learning Loop (Figure 3) supplies the course's week-by-week spine, and the return arc from Validating to Observing is the mechanism by which each cohort inherits the previous cohort's tightened constraints.

9 Evaluation Protocol

The most common failure mode in craft-technology literature is the unfalsifiable pilot: a small deployment, a set of favourable anecdotes, and no statement of what would have counted as failure. We therefore specify the evaluation in advance.

9.1 Design

A twelve-month, single-cluster, mixed-methods pilot with a within-cluster stepped-wedge structure. Participating households are randomised to entry cohort, so that households not yet onboarded serve as concurrent controls and each household additionally serves as its own historical control against a 90-day retrospective baseline. Target frame: 25–40 artisan households in one terracotta cluster, following the sampling conventions of existing cluster studies [29][30]. Qualitative strand: semi-structured interviews at months 0, 6 and 12, coded independently of the quantitative analysis.

9.3 Timeline

Disclosure commitment

Hypotheses, metrics and failure criteria are fixed before data collection. Results are to be published in full, including null and negative findings, and including the case in which artisans decline to continue using the platform — which we regard as the single most informative possible outcome.

10 Limitations and Ethical Considerations

No empirical validation yet

Every quantitative figure in this paper is either drawn from published work on adjacent tasks (Figure 6) or is an illustrative parameterisation of the formal model (Figure 5). PATHS has not been deployed. The framework's central claims are architectural and remain untested.

External validity of the borrowed evidence

The AR effect sizes come from industrial assembly with novice participants performing discrete, correct-or-incorrect steps [6][7]. Throwing a pot is continuous, expert, and has no single correct execution. It is entirely possible that an overlay which helps a novice assemble a toy hinders an expert who has thrown the same form for thirty years. The literature already signals this risk: benefits attenuate as tasks grow complex and head-mounted displays do not uniformly beat paper [8]. H2 is written to detect exactly this failure.

Digitisation as displacement

Heritage scholarship raises a standing objection: 3D archives can preserve the artefact while the practice dies, producing a well-documented extinction [11][12][14]. PATHS's answer is structural — the archive is a by-product of live commissions rather than a museum programme — but this is an argument, not a proof, and only longitudinal cluster data can settle it.

Extraction risk in captured process data

The same data that tightens φ could, in different hands, train a generative model to reproduce an artisan's hand without the artisan. The design commitment in §6.2 forbids this, but a commitment enforced only by its authors is weak. Cluster-held data governance, with the invariant set I(M) and the process corpus owned collectively by the cluster rather than by the platform, is the stronger arrangement and is the one we recommend for deployment.

Access and capability asymmetry

Shared cluster hardware removes the capital barrier but not the literacy one. Older artisans, and artisans in clusters with weak connectivity, may be systematically excluded — which would invert the SDG 10 claim entirely. Participation rates disaggregated by age, gender and literacy are therefore a reported outcome of the pilot, not an operational footnote.

Single-cluster generalisability

Indian terracotta practice varies substantially by region in clay body, firing method, motif grammar and market structure [30][31]. A result from one cluster does not transfer. Replication across at least three distinct clusters is required before any general claim.

11 Conclusion

PATHS is an argument that the interesting question for craft technology is not how much of the process to digitise but which parts, and that getting the boundary right matters more than getting the models good. The framework places computation on both sides of the making act — specification before, verification after — and forbids it inside. Everything else in the architecture follows from that single commitment: it is why the digital twin functions as a contract rather than a controller, why AR supplies a reference rather than a guide, why computer vision certifies rather than rejects, and why the digital multiplier is defined by its constraints rather than its generative capacity.

The framework's economic proposition is equally structural. It does not promise the artisan a larger number; it changes when payment arrives, who carries inventory risk, whose name the buyer knows, and who sets the price. Those are mechanisms that can be inspected in advance and measured afterwards, which is more than most craft-technology interventions offer.

What remains is to find out whether any of it survives contact with a working cluster. Section 9 specifies how we intend to find out and what would count as being wrong. The framework's value at this stage lies less in its claims than in its refutability: PATHS is stated precisely enough that a potter can render its central hypothesis false by putting the tablet down and walking back to the wheel.
